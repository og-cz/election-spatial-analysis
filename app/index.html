How often that province's local races (mayor, vice mayor, councilor) end in a landslide rather than a close contest, 2010–2025.

29% of local elections in the Philippines have ended in a landslide since 2010, and that share is rising almost everywhere. A clustering model looked at each town's vote margins and sorted every election into landslide or competitive on its own. It only measures how lopsided a result was, not who won or why.

        How this was determined
        YearPatternMayor shareMayor marginCouncilor ENCUnusual?

        "ENC" = effective number of candidates (1 ÷ Herfindahl–Hirschman index). A higher number means a more fragmented race. Landslide and competitive labels come from a k-means clustering model; "unusual" flags come from a separate isolation-forest anomaly-detection model. Both describe vote-share patterns only, not claims about irregularities or fraud.

        Methodology notes

        "Landslide" and "competitive" aren't manual labels. A clustering model grouped every town's election result into one of the two groups automatically, based only on how lopsided the result was. Every race in the selector above is its own independent model, fit only on that race's own vote shares and margins, never on winners or party. The Senate and Party List races elect multiple winners at once, so their split is closer to "how fragmented was the vote" than a landslide in the usual sense, and the app says so where it applies. Province figures are a straight share of that province's own locality-years, not a prediction and not weighted by population. Map boundaries are PSGC-derived provincial polygons. Six Cordillera provinces use a higher-detail extract because the default simplification distorted their shape (see the project's data notes). One BARMM administrative area (Special Geographic Area) has no polygon in this boundary set, so it's excluded from the map and from every figure on this page.
